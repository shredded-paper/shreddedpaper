In defense of structural realism

This article was adapted from a paper, therefore will not contain the usual unfunny gimmicks.

In the modern day, the illusion of a permanent American unipolarity, once engrained as the benevolent and savior nation of the world, has begun to fail and reveal the true anarchic nature of international order. The stability of this global system has been undermined by multiple compounding factors such as the failure of international institutions, the weaponization of economic globalization over finite resources, and the looming security dilemma. I believe the USA’s status as a world superpower is no longer as hegemonic as it once was, and by proxy proves the structural realist viewpoint as the dominant method of dissecting the now true multipolar international order.

Following the months after the Cold War began to die down, the western-world (mostly the USA) experienced an uptick in economic prosperity. At the time, American foreign policy operated under what John J. Mearsheimer refers to as “liberal triumphalism”, a delusional belief that integrating rising powers into global trade networks and international institutions would permanently turn them into peaceful, democratic stakeholders in an American led order. The world gleefully ate up the pipedream of a rules-based international order, headed by the world superpowers. This was America’s “unipolar moment”, and it did not last long, succumbing to the inevitable balancing forces that govern an anarchic world.

To be blunt, states cannot rely indefinitely on the benevolence of a single power. Intentions of a superpower state can change at any time, most commonly with a regime/government change, leaving states vulnerable to political whims it cannot control. Naturally out of common sense, rising powers seek to expand their own relative power and security without being indebted to the hegemon, possibly leaving themselves near defenseless against them. This dynamic is clearly illustrated by the economic rise of China, which was directly influenced by the USA in 2000/2001 when they sponsored China’s entry into the World Trade Organization. This permitted unrestricted flow of trade and capital, possibly driven by America’s delusional premise that economic growth would transform China into a docile, liberal stakeholder. However this had the opposite intention, and allowed China to modernize its armed forces and dominate global manufacturing.

While some scholars such as Stephen Brooks and William Wohlforth contend that the international system stays in a state of “partial unipolarity” because, on paper, the U.S. holds an overwhelming lead economically and security wise. They argue that true multipolarity requires multiple states to be roughly equal peers across every measurable category. While economic power is not the only category that needs to be analyzed to quantify this, economically successful states will naturally use their wealth. They inevitably convert their latent economic power into actual military power to protect their interests and expand their influence, better known as the concept of self-help. This can up tensions due to perceived power, how states actually perceive the distribution of power and calculate threat levels of other states.

The decline of the international order is clearly visible in the breakdown of global institutions and international law. Liberal thinkers like Daniel Deudney and G. John Ikenberry argued that the liberal order would last because bodies like the United Nations, the World Trade Organization, and international treaties were built to help nations handle shared Westphalian problems. In their view, nations would prefer shared rules over military conflict. In reality, global institutions cannot keep the peace when the interests of powerful nations don’t align. For example, the UN Security Council is routinely frozen by veto votes (held by permanent members China, USA, Russia, UK and France), leaving it helpless unless instances are truly for the benefit of all these members. Core international “rules” such as respecting another country's sovereignty are broken whenever a powerful nation decides a territorial invasion or economic coercion serves its own “survival”, often completely unprompted and simply used as a scapegoat. Even international trade is now weaponized more than ever through tariffs, sanctions, and supply chain blockades. The current trade war between the USA and Canada is a good example of this, with President Donald Trump simply attempting to strongarm their once biggest ally for more profits. While liberal institutionalists assume countries join these international orgs to achieve absolute gains where everyone receives a net benefit, Mearsheimer points out that under anarchy, states are destined to compete with each other, and will do what is best for the interest of that state alone. If a global rule or trade deal enables a rival to gain relative power faster, states will rationally reject or sandbag cooperation, break rules, or allow multilateral institutions to fall apart.

Beyond the issues with international orgs, the ongoing shift is pushed further partially due to the link between “endless” economic growth and the finite resources of the planet. The idea of commercial liberalism claimed that free trade and integration into the global market would quell the never-ending instances for military expansion, arguing that countries would be less likely to engage in wars and such for the benefit of mutual trade. However, modern geopolitical issues over energy security, critical minerals such as cobalt, and strategic trade routes demonstrates that market exchange has not eliminated power politics, instead it has added more fuel to the fire. Realist International Political Economy (IPE), which is rooted in mercantilism, shows that economic markets are driven by state power and national security priorities. When there's no global authority around to keep everyone “safe”, relying on other countries for energy, critical technology, or fragile supply chains puts a state at huge risk. This is why countries fall back on mercantilist tactics (protecting energy routes, raising tariffs) to keep their own power intact, but also seeking out other methods of acquisition to gain materials. As demand for fossil fuels and industrial minerals surges the competition over dwindling reserves triggers “resource wars”. For example, Russia has repeatedly exploited its status as an energy superpower by quote: “... using its control of natural gas supplies to Western Europe as a bargaining chip to mitigate the impact of sanctions”. Economic globalization has simply provided powerful states with new ways to hold resources over another countries’ head as a bargaining chip.

The main vector that cements the true anarchic order is the security dilemma, the idea that any military preparation, technological advancement, or alliance undertaken by one state for purely defensive reasons creates uncertainty in the minds of rival states. The opposing state does not truly know if these measures are intended strictly for defense or for future offensive action, possibly against them. Within structural realism, scholars disagree over how states rationally respond to these anarchic pressures, dividing into defensive realism and offensive realism. Defensive realists, argue that states are fundamentally security maximizers (defensive) rather than power maximizers (offensive). Many defensive realists warn that pursuing to accumulate overwhelming power is generally counterproductive, as it can automatically provoke rival states determined to protect their own survival. Once you begin to be a threat to nearby states, a state can’t just apologize and withdraw, as there would now be a constant fear that they might attempt to expand again.

Conversely, offensive realists, led by John J. Mearsheimer, offers a far more competitive prediction, contending that states are power maximizers driven by the anarchic structure of the system to continuously accumulate relative power. Mearsheimer contends that because states can never know exactly how much power is enough to guarantee survival, the safest strategy under anarchy is to maximize relative power as much as possible. Consequently, great powers do not settle for “good deals” or true balanced equality with other states, they actively look for opportunities to gain power at the expense of potential competitors. The volatility of the security dilemma in a multipolar world is further complicated by the dynamics of nukes and WMDs (Weapons of Mass Destruction), with even smaller nations not once considered “threats” now holding nuclear capability. However, they could instead bring further international stability due to“Mutually Assured Destruction”. But as military technology advances, the security dilemma raises tensions and becomes far more dangerous (now compounded with nukes and drone warfare), perhaps eventually getting to the point where it could trigger direct great-power war.

Until a significant shift occurs, global politics will likely remain defined by great-power rivalry, perhaps between China and the USA, rather than shared international rules.
